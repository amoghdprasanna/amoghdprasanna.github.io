## Motivation

A qudit holds more than two levels, so one transmon can do the work of several qubits. The catch is compilation. A target unitary on $d$ levels has to become a sequence of operations the hardware actually offers, and the hardware has its own opinions: the levels are not evenly spaced, and population leaks into states outside the computational subspace. Analytic decompositions exist, but they are not written with those imperfections in mind.

## Approach

The native operations come from the Jaynes-Cummings interaction between a transmon and a cavity mode,

$$
H = \omega_c\, a^\dagger a + \sum_j \omega_j \lvert j\rangle\langle j\rvert + g\,\bigl(a^\dagger b + a\, b^\dagger\bigr),
\qquad b = \sum_j \sqrt{j+1}\, \lvert j\rangle\langle j+1\rvert ,
$$

where the uneven spacing of the $\omega_j$ is the anharmonicity. Together with drives on the transmon, this interaction is universal for controlling the joint system. I train models to choose sequences of these native operations for a target $U \in \mathrm{SU}(d)$, and the training objective scores the whole sequence, including how much population leaves the computational subspace along the way. Every learned decomposition is benchmarked against analytic baselines on the same targets.
